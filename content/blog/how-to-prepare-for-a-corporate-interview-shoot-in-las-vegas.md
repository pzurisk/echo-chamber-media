A good interview on camera is mostly decided before anyone presses record. The room, the prep, and the schedule do more for the final video than any piece of gear. Here is what to sort out ahead of time so the shoot day goes smoothly and the person on camera looks and sounds like themselves.

## Pick the room for sound first

People choose interview rooms with their eyes. Choose with your ears. We can light almost any room, but we can't remove an air handler that hums through every answer.

Stand in the room for a minute with the door closed and listen. Check for:

- Air conditioning you can't switch off for the length of the interview
- A hallway, break room, or elevator on the other side of the wall
- Hard floors and bare walls that make voices echo
- Phones, printers, and anything else that rings or beeps on its own

In Las Vegas, add one more: casino floors and convention halls are loud everywhere, all day. If your interview happens during a show or at a resort, book a meeting room or a suite away from the floor instead of trying to shoot in the middle of it.

## Then look at the space

Bigger is better, and deeper is better than wider. Distance between the person and the wall behind them is what gives the shot some depth. A small office with the subject pressed against a wall will always look flat.

A few things that help:

- A room where we can pull the person several feet off the back wall
- Something real in the background (your shop floor, your product, your lobby) instead of a blank wall
- Windows you can cover. Daylight shifts during a long interview, and blinds let us keep the look consistent from the first answer to the last.

If you're not sure, send photos of two or three rooms and we'll tell you which one to use.

## Prep the person, not a script

The fastest way to get a stiff interview is to hand someone a script. Most people can't read lines and still sound like themselves.

What works better:

- **Send the topics a few days ahead.** Topics, not word-for-word questions. Nobody likes surprises on camera.
- **Tell them it's a conversation.** They'll talk to a person sitting next to the lens, not into the camera.
- **Let them know mistakes are fine.** We can stop, restart an answer, and cut around anything. Nothing is live.
- **Ask for full sentences.** If the question gets cut from the edit, "About six years" means nothing. "We've been in Las Vegas about six years" stands on its own.

## What to wear

- Solid colors that fit well
- No tight stripes or small checks, which can shimmer on camera
- No jewelry that clinks, since the microphone hears all of it
- A second option on a hanger, in case the first one blends into the background
- If logo shirts matter to you, bring clean ones for everyone on camera

## Block more time than the interview itself

The interview is the short part. Lighting and audio get built before the first person sits down, and that takes time to do properly. Ask your crew how long setup will take in your room and schedule around that number.

A few scheduling habits that pay off:

- **Give the crew the room early**, before the people on camera arrive
- **Put your busiest person first**, so a late meeting doesn't push them off the schedule
- **Leave gaps between people.** Back-to-back interviews run long, and the last person ends up rushed.
- **Plan for B-roll.** Footage of your team working, your space, and your product is what the edit cuts to while someone is talking. It needs its own slot on the schedule.
